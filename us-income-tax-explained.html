US Income Tax Explained (2026)

This guide explains how US income tax works, including federal tax brackets, the standard deduction, Social Security, Medicare, and the role of state income tax. If you want to understand why two people on the same salary can take home very different amounts, this page is the place to start.

The United States does not use a single flat nationwide income tax. Instead, take-home pay is shaped by a mix of federal tax, payroll tax, state tax, filing status, and pretax deductions such as a 401(k).

This page is a simplified guide for salary planning and comparison. Real tax outcomes can differ because of filing status, credits, dependents, itemized deductions, retirement contributions, healthcare deductions, bonuses, RSUs, and local taxes.

How US income tax works

1) Gross income

Your starting point is your gross pay: salary, wages, overtime, bonus, and in some cases other taxable compensation.

2) Taxable income

Federal taxable income is generally lower than gross income because deductions such as the standard deduction and some pretax retirement contributions reduce the amount being taxed.

3) Take-home pay

Your final net pay can be reduced by federal tax, Social Security, Medicare, state income tax, local tax, 401(k) contributions, healthcare deductions, and other payroll items.

One of the biggest misunderstandings around US tax is the idea that your whole salary is taxed at your top bracket. That is not how it works. The federal system is progressive, which means each slice of taxable income is taxed at the rate for that slice rather than one flat rate on the entire amount.

That is why a person on $70,000 does not suddenly lose 22% or 24% of their entire pay just because they move into a higher bracket. Only the portion of taxable income inside that bracket is taxed at that higher rate.

Your top bracket is not the same as your effective tax rate. Effective tax rate is the share of your total income that actually goes to tax after the lower bands and deductions have been applied.

2026 standard deduction

The standard deduction is one of the biggest reasons take-home pay is better than many people first assume. It reduces how much of your income is exposed to federal income tax before the bracket system is applied.

  • Single: $16,100
  • Married filing jointly: $32,200
  • Head of household: $24,150

Many employees use the standard deduction rather than itemizing, especially when doing quick salary and take-home comparisons.

Simple example

If a single filer earns $70,000, the full $70,000 is not exposed to federal income tax in the same way. The standard deduction reduces taxable income before the bracket calculation is made.

This is one of the reasons a salary page or calculator should always show a breakdown rather than just applying one flat percentage to gross pay.

FICA: Social Security and Medicare

Social Security

Social Security tax is charged at 6.2% on employee wages up to the yearly wage base. For 2026, that wage base is $184,500.

Once wages go above the Social Security cap, that part of FICA no longer rises through Social Security, although Medicare still continues.

Medicare

Medicare tax is charged at 1.45% on employee wages and does not have the same regular wage cap as Social Security.

High earners can also face extra Medicare withholding in some cases, which is another reason exact net pay can differ from simplified examples.

Why state taxes matter so much

Federal tax is only part of the picture. In the US, state income tax can create a major take-home difference between people on the same salary. A worker on $70,000 in Texas can have a different net outcome from someone on $70,000 in California or New York even before local taxes and deductions are considered.

California

Progressive state tax structure. Take-home pay is often lower than no-tax states on the same gross salary.

Texas & Florida

No state income tax, which can make headline salary go further, all else being equal.

New York & Illinois

New York can be heavier than many states, while Illinois is commonly treated as a flatter state-tax comparison point.

This is why state salary pages are useful: they turn a general tax explanation into a specific take-home answer for the salary band and state the user actually cares about.

What affects take-home pay beyond headline tax rates?

401(k) contributions

Pretax retirement contributions can reduce taxable income for federal purposes, while also reducing the cash you take home each payday.

Bonus and overtime

Extra pay can move more of your income into higher marginal brackets, and withholding on bonuses can look different from regular salary withholding.

Healthcare and payroll deductions

Insurance, HSA or FSA contributions, commuter benefits, and other payroll deductions can materially change real take-home pay.

Filing status

Single, married filing jointly, and head of household all change the tax calculation and can shift the effective tax rate noticeably.

Local taxes

City or local taxes can further reduce pay in some locations, which means state-only comparisons do not always tell the whole story.

Tax credits and deductions

Credits and special deductions can pull actual tax below what a broad salary example suggests, which is why guides and calculators should be treated as estimates.

US income tax FAQ

How does the US federal tax bracket system work?
The federal system is progressive. Different slices of taxable income are taxed at different rates. You do not pay your top marginal rate on every dollar you earn.
What is the difference between gross pay and taxable income?
Gross pay is your headline income before deductions. Taxable income is the amount left after deductions such as the standard deduction and some pretax contributions have been applied.
Why can two people on the same salary take home different amounts?
They may live in different states, have different filing statuses, contribute different amounts to retirement, pay local taxes, or have different payroll deductions such as healthcare premiums.
Is FICA separate from federal income tax?
Yes. FICA generally refers to Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes. These are separate from federal income tax and appear in addition to it on most salary calculations.
Do Texas and Florida really have no state income tax?
They do not have a regular state income tax on wages, which is one reason salary comparisons often show stronger net pay in those states than in higher-tax states.
