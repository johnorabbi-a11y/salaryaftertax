Take Home Pay US (2026)

Your take-home pay in the US is your salary after federal tax, payroll tax, and in many cases state income tax. This guide explains what reduces net pay, why take-home pay varies by state, and how salary, filing status, and 401(k) contributions can change your final paycheck.

Two people can earn the same salary and still take home very different amounts. That is because net pay is shaped by a mix of federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, state tax, local tax, retirement contributions, and payroll deductions.

This page is designed for salary planning and comparison. Real net pay can differ because of W-4 settings, benefits, healthcare deductions, itemized deductions, tax credits, RSUs, local taxes, and employer payroll treatment.

What is take-home pay in the US?

Take-home pay is the amount left from your gross pay after deductions. For most employees in the US, the biggest deductions are federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and in many states an additional state income tax.

That means your headline salary is not the same as your real spendable income. A $70,000 salary does not mean you actually receive $70,000 in your bank account. The gap between gross pay and net pay is what this guide is about.

Gross pay

Your starting salary or wages before tax and before other payroll deductions.

Net pay

The amount left after tax and selected payroll deductions. This is your true take-home pay.

Why it varies

Filing status, state, retirement contributions, and benefits can all change the final result.

How federal income tax affects take-home pay

Federal income tax is progressive. That means different slices of taxable income are taxed at different rates rather than one flat rate on your whole salary.

Before those brackets are applied, most people reduce their taxable income with the standard deduction. For 2026, the standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers, $32,200 for married filing jointly, and $24,150 for head of household. Because of that, federal taxable income is often lower than people expect when they first look at their salary.

Your top bracket is not your effective tax rate. Only the income inside each bracket is taxed at that bracket’s rate, so real federal tax is usually lower than people think when they first hear “I’m in the 22% bracket” or “I’m in the 24% bracket.”

FICA: Social Security and Medicare

Social Security

Social Security is usually charged at 6.2% on employee wages up to the annual wage base. For 2026, that wage base is $184,500.

Once wages move above the Social Security wage base, that part of payroll tax no longer grows through Social Security.

Medicare

Medicare is usually charged at 1.45% on covered wages. Unlike Social Security, regular Medicare tax does not use the same wage cap.

This is why higher earners still see Medicare continuing even after Social Security stops rising.

Why state taxes change take-home pay so much

State income tax is one of the biggest reasons identical salaries can lead to very different net pay outcomes. A worker in Texas or Florida can often keep more of the same gross salary than someone in California or New York because Texas and Florida do not impose a regular state income tax on wages.

California

Progressive state tax system that can noticeably reduce take-home pay compared with no-tax states.

Texas and Florida

No regular state income tax on wages, which is why net pay comparisons often look stronger there.

New York and Illinois

New York can be heavier for take-home pay, while Illinois is often treated as a flatter state comparison point.

Other things that reduce take-home pay

401(k) contributions

Pretax retirement contributions can reduce federal taxable income while also reducing the amount of cash that hits your paycheck.

Healthcare deductions

Employer health insurance, dental plans, HSA or FSA contributions, and other benefits can reduce net pay.

Bonus and overtime

Extra income changes withholding, shifts more income into higher marginal bands, and can make take-home pay look less linear than basic salary comparisons suggest.

Filing status

Single, married filing jointly, and head of household can produce meaningfully different federal tax results.

Local tax

Some areas impose extra city or local tax, which means state-only comparisons do not always tell the full story.

Tax credits and special deductions

Credits, itemized deductions, and other personal tax factors can push actual take-home pay above or below quick salary estimates.

Take-home pay US FAQ

What does take-home pay mean in the US?
Take-home pay is the money left from your gross wages or salary after tax and other payroll deductions have been taken out.
Why is my take-home pay lower than my salary suggests?
Federal tax, Social Security, Medicare, state tax, retirement contributions, healthcare deductions, and other payroll items all reduce net pay.
Do federal tax brackets mean my whole salary is taxed at one rate?
No. Federal tax is progressive, so different slices of taxable income are taxed at different rates. Your top bracket is not the rate applied to your entire income.
Why do people in some states keep more of the same salary?
State income tax is a major reason. Texas and Florida do not have a regular state income tax on wages, while states like California and New York can reduce net pay more heavily.
Does a 401(k) reduce take-home pay?
Yes. A 401(k) contribution usually lowers the cash that lands in your paycheck, even though it can also reduce federal taxable income.
Is this page enough to calculate my exact paycheck?
No. This page explains the system and the main drivers of take-home pay. For a closer estimate, use the linked salary, monthly, state, and calculator pages across the site.
