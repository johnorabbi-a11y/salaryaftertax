Earning £22 per hour in the UK works out at roughly £45,760 per year if you work a standard 40-hour week across the full year. After Income Tax and National Insurance, estimated take-home pay is about £35,408 per year, or around £2,951 per month.

How much is £22 an hour per year?

A rate of £22 an hour typically equals £45,760 per year when based on a 40-hour working week for 52 weeks. That puts it around the mid-range of full-time UK salaries and makes it a useful comparison point against common salaried roles in the £43k to £46k bracket.

Because this is above the personal allowance and into the main tax-paying range, the difference between gross pay and real take-home pay becomes important. Looking only at the hourly rate can make earnings look higher than they actually feel once deductions come off your payslip.

What affects take-home pay on £22 an hour?

The estimate above is a strong baseline, but real take-home pay can move up or down depending on your personal setup and how your employer handles deductions.

Pension contributions
Workplace pension payments can reduce taxable income and change your net pay.
Student loan
Student loan repayments can take a noticeable amount from monthly pay.
Overtime and shifts
Extra hours can lift gross earnings, but deductions rise with them too.
Tax code changes
A different tax code can increase or reduce how much pay you keep.
Bonuses
One-off bonus payments can make a month look stronger but tax more heavily.
Scottish tax bands
Scottish taxpayers use different income tax bands, so results can differ.

Is £22 an hour a good wage in the UK?

For many workers, £22 an hour is a solid hourly rate. It is comfortably above minimum wage levels and can produce a full-time income of nearly £46,000 a year before tax. In many parts of the UK, that can support a decent standard of living, especially compared with lower hourly rates in the £15 to £18 range.

That said, what feels “good” depends heavily on housing costs, family size, commuting costs and whether you have major deductions coming off your wage. Someone renting in a cheaper area may feel comfortable on this income, while someone with high childcare or housing costs may still need to budget closely.

This is why comparing the hourly rate with real monthly take-home pay matters more than looking at the gross figure alone.
